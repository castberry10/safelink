# Codebook for Written Rationale
This codebook presents findings from the SafeLink Phase 2 user study (N=30 respondents, 300 responses), analyzing participants' written rationales for their judgments on Warning-category URLs.
## Classification Scheme and Response Distribution

| Category | Definition | Ground Truth | User Response | n |
|----------|-----------|--------------|---------------|---|
| **TP (Warning-phishing → Phishing)** | Correctly identified a phishing site as phishing | Yes | Yes | 118 |
| **FN (Warning-phishing → Safe)** | Incorrectly judged a phishing site as safe | Yes | No | 32 |
| **TN (Warning-benign → Safe)** | Correctly judged a non-phishing site as not phishing | No | No | 91 |
| **FP (Warning-benign → Phishing)** | Incorrectly judged a non-phishing site as phishing | No | Yes | 59 |

# 1. Codebook for Correct Phishing Identification (Warning-phishing correctly judged as phishing, n=118)


| **Code** | **Code Description** | **SubCode** | **SubCode Description** | **Freq.** | **Examples** |
|----------|----------------------|-------------|--------------------------|-----------|--------------|
| **Explicit use of impersonation cues** | Participants directly quoted or restated impersonation-related expressions in the SafeLink explanation, applying them to the phishing definition. The strongest and most frequent cue for correct judgment. | **Explicit recognition of impersonation intent** | Direct quotation or restatement of expressions such as "tries to impersonate," "is not the real ~," "imitates," "impersonation," applied to the phishing definition. | 39 | "the part where it says it is trying to impersonate" <br> "Because the description says it is impersonating a real service." |
| | | **Direct quotation of negative declarative statements** | Adopted SafeLink's negative declarative phrases such as "this is not the actual bank website" verbatim as the basis for phishing judgment. Suggests that declarative statements were more effective than hedged tone. | 13 | "Because of the description that says 'This is not the actual bank website.'" <br> "It is not the actual bank website, and they are trying to extract login information, so I judged it as phishing." |
| | | **Recognition of typosquatting / character variation** | Identified subtle character variations such as 'att' mimicking AT&T. Judgment originating from brand recognition. | 4 | "There are slight character changes intended to deceive — phishing." <br> "It is leading users to believe it is at&t." |
| **Domain / infrastructure mismatch cues** | Used mismatches between URL and page content, or infrastructure cues not used by legitimate institutions, as decisive evidence of phishing. | **Recognition of domain–content mismatch** | Used SafeLink-stated information such as "the address and content do not match" or "the domain differs from the real one" as a decisive cue for the URL–content mismatch. | 20 | "Because of the content saying the address and content do not match." <br> "The address and content don't match, and there is even an install button — even more suspicious." |
| | | **Infrastructure cues (dynamic DNS / free hosting)** | Combined SafeLink-stated unofficial infrastructure such as "dyndns.org" or "weeblysite.com" with the user's own knowledge that legitimate institutions do not use such services to support phishing judgment. | 10 | "It seems to bypass phishing detection through dynamic DNS." <br> "Given the address 'myweb.dyndns.org' which appears to be created at an individual level, I think it is highly likely a phishing site." |
| **Recognition of credential and action-induction risks** | Recognized cues that the page attempts credential entry or directs users to external channels as direct evidence of phishing. | **Login form / credential-collection cues** | Recognized credential-collection attempts such as "Login ID," "Login Password," "username/password entry" as strong phishing evidence. Particularly prominent in the Arvest Bank impersonation item. | 15 | "It impersonates a bank site and asks for ID and password — strong phishing evidence, so it is phishing." <br> "It is built to imitate a real bank website, prominently featuring ID and password entry fields to deceive users and steal personal information, therefore it is a phishing site." |
| | | **Recognition of external-channel induction** | Recognized cues that direct user behavior — such as Telegram channel join buttons or redirects to external sites — as part of phishing techniques. | 7 | "I judge it as phishing because it directs users to an external chatroom." <br> "Because the description said it induces Telegram channel joins and tries to attract users." |
| **Surface-anomaly heuristics** | Without recognizing concrete impersonation mechanisms, relied on surface signals such as foreign language, unusual expressions, or "phishing possibility" to make conservative phishing judgments. | **Foreign-language / non-standard character cues** | In the Telegram impersonation item, the Kazakh/Russian text mentioned by SafeLink was perceived as an unusual signal and used to judge the site as phishing. | 11 | "Given that the inspection result contains Russian text, I thought it would not be a typical site." <br> "Unfamiliar foreign-language use, and personal suspicion of the Telegram service." |
| | | **Adoption of probabilistic phrasing ("phishing possibility")** | Accepted SafeLink's hedged phrase "there is a possibility of phishing" itself as sufficient grounds. Reflects a conservative decision-making tendency. | 6 | "Because they said there is a phishing possibility…" <br> "If they say there is a phishing possibility, I think it is phishing." |
| | | **Non-specific suspicion / heuristic judgment** | Without citing concrete cues, relied on abstract impressionistic judgments such as "looks like an illegal site," "phishing-vibe," "suspicious." Cases of judgment based on simple heuristics without reading the explanation in depth. | 9 | "Looks like an illegal site." <br> "Has a phishing-like feel." |

# 2. Codebook for Missed Phishing (Warning-phishing incorrectly judged as safe, n=32)


| **Code** | **Code Description** | **SubCode** | **SubCode Description** | **Freq.** | **Examples** |
|----------|----------------------|-------------|--------------------------|-----------|--------------|
| **Surface-meaning misreading of explanation lexicon** | Interpreted certain words in the SafeLink explanation only by their surface meaning, mistakenly receiving expressions originally intended as risk signals as safety signals. The largest source of error in this category. | **Misreading "preparing" / "incomplete site" as a trust signal** | Participants received SafeLink's expression "the website is still under preparation" used in item 10 as a benign signal or a signal of suspended risk. The largest cause of FN in this study. | 9 | "Based on the analysis result, it seems the site is still under preparation, so it is not a phishing site." <br> "Because it just looks like an unfinished website." |
| | | **Misreading "Protected" as a protection signal** | Misunderstood the word "Protected" in the item 10 explanation as a safety signal indicating that the site protects user information. Semantic confusion based on the surface meaning of the English word. | 3 | "It seems to be a protected site." <br> "I think the word 'protected' means it is safe." |
| **Narrow application of the impersonation definition** | As a result of strictly applying the definition in the pre-survey instructions, participants concluded the site was not phishing whenever explicit, direct impersonation cues were absent. They followed the definition correctly, but the SafeLink explanation failed to highlight impersonation cues sufficiently. | **Judgment based on absence of impersonation cues (strict application of definition)** | By strictly applying the "impersonation" criterion of the phishing definition, participants concluded a site was not phishing when explicit impersonation cues were absent. They followed the definition correctly, but SafeLink's explanation failed to express impersonation cues sufficiently. | 8 | "Does not impersonate." <br> "Doesn't really seem to be doing any phishing." |
| | | **Distinguishing explicit impersonation from legitimate-service use** | In item 6 (Telegram channel join), participants did not apply the phishing definition because Telegram is a legitimate service and the page does not imitate Telegram itself. An error arising from the subtle boundary of the definition. | 3 | "Joining a Telegram channel itself is not copying Telegram but rather happens legitimately within the Telegram service, so I don't think it is phishing." <br> "The unusual phrases and the Join Channel button seemed to aim more at illegal advertising than at deceiving users, so I judged it as not phishing." |
| | | **Judgment based on absence of credential-entry cues** | In item 9 (Steam impersonation) and similar items, the absence of direct credential-input forms was used as grounds for low phishing likelihood. | 2 | "Even if a user reaches a different site than intended, they are unlikely to enter personal information, so I don't think it is phishing." <br> "The domain and description differ [but it is not phishing]." |
| **Weakening of warnings by familiarity with named brands** | Cases where a known brand (Steam) explicitly named in the SafeLink explanation neutralized the domain-mismatch warning. Brand recognition overrode other risk cues. | **Trust based on recognition of known brand (Steam item)** | In item 9, participants interpreted SafeLink's mention of "Steam page" as a normal-brand signal. The domain-mismatch warning was weakened by brand familiarity. | 3 | "Since Steam is a verified site, it is probably not phishing." <br> "It is a page of the famous game Steam." |
| **Appeal to insufficient explanation evidence** | Concluded the site was not phishing because they considered SafeLink's explanation insufficient to claim phishing. A case where insufficient explanation directly induced error. | **Appeal to lack of grounds / weak information** | Concluded a site was not phishing on the grounds that SafeLink's explanation does not contain sufficient phishing cues. A case where insufficient explanatory information directly caused FN. | 4 | "There are no grounds." <br> "I couldn't find information saying it is a fake site." |

# 3. Codebook for Correct Benign Acceptance (Warning-benign correctly judged as safe, n=91)

| **Code** | **Code Description** | **SubCode** | **SubCode Description** | **Freq.** | **Examples** |
|----------|----------------------|-------------|--------------------------|-----------|--------------|
| **Definition-aligned reasoning** | Accurately applied the definition in the pre-survey instructions ("whether impersonation is involved"), actively confirming the absence of impersonation cues and distinguishing illegality from phishing. The most frequent and accurate judgment pattern. | **Explicit confirmation of absence of impersonation cues** | Actively confirmed that impersonation/disguise-related expressions are absent in the SafeLink explanation, and adopted this — based on the definition — as grounds for non-phishing judgment. The most frequent and accurate inferential pattern. | 26 | "I thought it is not a phishing site because it does not impersonate other sites I know to lure access." <br> "It does not disguise itself as another site, so it cannot be considered phishing." |
| | | **Recognition of illegal ≠ phishing distinction** | Acknowledged that the site is illegal (gambling, copyright infringement) while explicitly applying the definitional distinction that this does not in itself mean phishing. | 25 | "It is an illegal site, but there is no phishing-related text, so it is not phishing." <br> "Although it is an illegal site, it doesn't seem like personal information would be stolen." |
| | | **Confirmation of domain–content match** | Used the fact that the URL and site content match — as stated in the SafeLink explanation — as evidence of absence of impersonation. | 4 | "Since the URL and the site name are identical, it is not phishing." <br> "The text and the domain match, so it does not seem to be phishing." |
| **Recognition of clarity in site purpose / function** | Judged that SafeLink's explanation clearly conveys the site's intended use (sharing gambling results, promoting movie/music downloads), so no intent to deceive is visible. | **Citing the explicitly stated site purpose as evidence of non-impersonation** | Used the fact that SafeLink's explanation specifies a single intended use (gambling-result sharing, music-download promotion, etc.) as grounds for the absence of impersonation. | 14 | "Although I read in the inspection result that this is a private gambling site, since it just manages gambling results and does not request any information from users, I thought it is not phishing." <br> "Rather than phishing, it focuses on promoting an online gambling site." |
| | | **Recognition of absence of personal-information-collection cues** | Judged the site as not phishing on the grounds that it does not attempt personal-information entry or credential collection. Some respondents acknowledged indirect risks via advertisements, however. | 5 | "I thought it would not request any information from users." <br> "I detected no impersonation of any site and no attempt to obtain personal information." |
| | | **Acknowledgment of advertising / external-link risk while denying direct phishing** | A nuanced distinction that the site itself is not phishing, but hosted advertisements or external links may carry risk. Appeared in responses with relatively higher security knowledge. | 3 | "The site does not directly ask for personal information; it is the advertisements they host that often request user information, so caution is needed... but the site itself does not attempt phishing, so it is not a phishing site." <br> "It seems to generate revenue by advertising gambling while listing various illegal-site links, so it is not phishing." |
| **Brief / skeptical negation responses** | Without detailed reasoning, denied phishing on the basis of a short negation expression or an evaluation of the strength of the explanation. | **Brief negation responses (definition-applied trust)** | Concisely stated only that no phishing cues are visible in the SafeLink explanation, using short responses such as "no grounds" or "no phishing-related content." Cases of applying the definition without detailed reasoning. | 11 | "No grounds." <br> "No phishing-related content." |
| | | **Judgment based on skepticism toward weak explanations** | Considered the SafeLink explanation too weak to claim phishing, and consequently denied phishing. Indirectly, participants are evaluating the strength of the explanation itself. | 2 | "I thought the explanation that it is a private gambling site looked weak, so I judged it as not phishing." <br> "Since the meaning of the text cannot be interpreted, the claim that it is a phishing site looked weak, so I judged it as not phishing." |

# 4. Codebook for False Phishing Alarm (Warning-benign incorrectly judged as phishing, n=59)


| **Code** | **Code Description** | **SubCode** | **SubCode Description** | **Freq.** | **Examples** |
|----------|----------------------|-------------|--------------------------|-----------|--------------|
| **Generalization of secondary risk language as phishing** | General security warnings that SafeLink included in benign-site explanations ("malicious ads," "malicious software," "be careful," "warning") were interpreted by participants as direct phishing signals. The largest cause of error in this category. | **Generalization of risk-related secondary phrases as phishing** | Interpreted general security warnings such as "malicious ads," "malicious software downloads," "malicious code," "you should be careful" — phrases included in SafeLink's explanation — as direct phishing signals. | 18 | "Because inducing downloads can be used as a channel for phishing, it is phishing." <br> "It seems to distribute malicious code through downloads." |
| | | **Treating the 'Warning' label itself as a risk signal** | Interpreted SafeLink's 'Warning' label itself as indicating phishing possibility. The label functions as a conservative signal, but participants did not distinguish the definitional differences within it. | 5 | "The result showed Warning, and there was a description that it is a private gambling site." <br> "The Warning came up, and there was text saying it is being used to promote an online gambling site." |
| | | **Generalization of 'Download Now' buttons / action-induction as phishing** | Interpreted download buttons or action-inducing phrases as part of phishing techniques, although these are in fact natural UI elements of legitimate download sites. | 7 | "The 'Download Now' text seems suspicious." <br> "It is not a download site itself but a site that promotes downloads, with phrases like 'Download Now' that easily lead to other links — I thought there is a phishing intent." |
| **Illegality = phishing equivalence error** | Failed to apply the core distinction in the pre-survey instructions ("non-impersonating illegal is not phishing") and equated illegality itself — gambling, illegal downloads — with phishing. | **Schematic equation of illegality with phishing** | A cognitive error that equates illegality itself — gambling, illegal downloads — with phishing. Responses that failed to apply the core distinction in the pre-survey instructions (non-impersonating illegal is not phishing). | 12 | "I think gambling-related sites are phishing sites." <br> "Just being an online gambling site already gives it a phishing smell." |
| | | **Skepticism toward 'free' expressions** | Considered "free download" and "free music" expressions as not normal, interpreting free provision itself as bait for fraud or phishing. | 9 | "Since it is a free download, access is easy and people get caught easily — I think it is phishing." <br> "The 'free music' element lures with the idea of free, so it would easily steal information." |
| **Lexical / linguistic suspicion** | Used general suspicion of foreign languages or impressionistic expressions — rather than concrete impersonation cues — as grounds for phishing judgment. | **Suspicion of unintelligible foreign language / unclear meaning** | In items 3, 5, etc., participants perceived the existence of phrases they could not interpret (e.g., Indonesian) as a threat signal in itself. | 5 | "It uses words I don't know that are hard to interpret, so people might just pass over them carelessly." <br> "I cannot understand the meaning of the text, but I infer it from the fact that it leads to an online gambling site." |
| | | **Broad heuristic / conservative avoidance** | Without specific cues, participants made avoidance-oriented phishing judgments based on impressions such as "suspicious," "weird," or "uneasy." Reliance on general suspicion without applying the definition. | 3 | "It seems suspicious that it is a gambling-related site. I have heard online gambling scams take your money and run." <br> "I don't know what Tamil movies are, but I thought illegal download sites might leak information in unknown ways." |
