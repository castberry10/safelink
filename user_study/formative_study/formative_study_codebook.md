# User Feedback Codebook 

This codebook reports the inductive coding results of semi-structured interview responses collected from the SafeLink Phase 1 user study (N=12 in-person interviews, P1–P12).

---

| **Code** | **Code Description** | **SubCode** | **SubCode Description** | **Freq.** | **Examples** |
|----------|----------------------|-------------|--------------------------|-----------|--------------|
| **1. Explanation Quality and Trust Formation** | User evaluation of SafeLink's risk-rationale explanations. Captures how clear and logical reasoning, link previews, and learning effects influence user decision-making and system trust. Includes both positive aspects (persuasiveness, learning effects) and negative aspects (insufficient length, fragmentation, hedged tone). | **Risk rationale supports decision-making** | Responses indicating that providing concrete rationales (e.g., potential personal-information leakage, common scam techniques) beyond a simple Safe/Warning/Danger label helped users understand and accept the system's judgment. | 14 | "Normally I can't tell what a link contains just by looking at it, but with the risk level shown alongside the rationale, it definitely helped." <br> "When the feedback says something like 'your personal information could be stolen,' the system clearly explains why it's risky, which helped me decide." |
| | | **Site preview / advance information** | Positive evaluation of being able to understand a page's purpose and nature without directly visiting the link. Responses that information volume increased compared to URL-only judgment, reducing decision burden. | 8 | "It was good that the app explained what role the URL plays and what purpose the site was built for, so I could decide whether to access or block it without going in. It was nice having a kind of preview." <br> "Even without accessing the link directly, the system showed information about the page and its risk level, which built trust." |
| | | **Learning effects through scam-pattern examples** | Responses that explanations of how typical phishing/scam techniques operate were persuasive. Evaluations that the system goes beyond simple warnings to provide knowledge users can apply to similar future situations. | 3 | "Showing how scams typically work, like 'this website uses techniques commonly employed by scammers' — that came across well. It was persuasive." <br> "The use of examples was really good. 'This is the kind of phishing technique used' — those examples were great." |
| | | **Insufficient explanation length / fragmentation** | Responses that the explanations provided were too short or fragmented to make a confident judgment. Requests for more concrete and detailed information. | 8 | "The information was too short. I wished there was more explanation. The information given was too fragmented." <br> "I liked that the system provided a brief introduction to the site, but because it was so brief, I couldn't get detailed information, which was disappointing." |
| | | **Aversion to hedged tone** | Responses that hedged expressions such as "appears to be ~" or "might be ~" weakened system confidence and lowered trust. The ambiguity was particularly sensitive in financial contexts. | 7 | "The tone, the explanation — 'it appears to be ~' — felt too unclear, which made the system seem less trustworthy. 'It looks like ~' doesn't give me certainty." <br> "Because it was finance-related, I had to be sensitive to tone. Visually and logically I could tell it was safe, but because the tone was ambiguous, I gave it -1." |
| | | **Lack of structured presentation** | Critique that explanations were provided only as plain prose, making key information hard to grasp at a glance. Suggestion that keyword highlighting or structured formats would improve readability. | 3 | "The explanation isn't structured — it's just prose, so I wondered if people would actually read it. People only look at the topic sentence and skim the rest." <br> "If the visualization showed key keywords instead of just plain prose, wouldn't that be better?" |
| | | **Insufficient accessibility for non-experts** | Critique that some terms or expressions are difficult for general users without background knowledge to understand. Need raised for plain language for older adults and general users. | 2 | "The part that says 'this seems to be made by ~' — I gave it -1 because I wondered whether people without background knowledge could understand it." <br> "Terms and words that non-experts and people without related knowledge can also understand should be used." |
| **2. Evaluation of Three-Tier Policy** | Influence of the three-tier (Safe/Warning/Danger) labeling structure on user perception and decision-making. Includes user opinions on the existence of the Warning tier itself, clarity of classification criteria, and consistency of explanation quality across tiers. | **Value of the Warning tier (complementing the binary)** | Positive evaluation that 'Warning' fills the gray zone that a Safe/Danger binary cannot capture, making the system feel more trustworthy and appropriately delegating judgment authority to users. Often expressed via the traffic-light analogy. | 6 | "It's like a traffic light — for the yellow light, you decide whether to go, and the individual takes responsibility… having Warning actually felt better to me." <br> "A binary mindset is somewhat dangerous. But adding the Warning tier let users make their own judgment again, which was good." |
| | | **Satisfaction with Safe/Danger explanation quality** | Positive evaluation, in contrast to the Warning tier, that the classification and explanations for Safe or Danger tiers were clear and sufficient. Particularly, the Danger classification and its rationale built trust. | 5 | "For Safe and Danger cases, the explanations were clear and definitive, which I liked." <br> "Safe/Danger became indicators I could absolutely trust, while 'Warning' became an indicator that I should be careful on my own." |
| | | **Ambiguity in Warning classification criteria** | Responses that both seemingly risky and seemingly safe sites were classified as 'Warning,' making the criteria feel inconsistent. Led to doubts about reliability. | 5 | "At a glance, some sites looked safe but received Warning, while others that anyone would consider risky also received Warning, so the criteria seemed even more ambiguous." <br> "Some sites that looked somewhat risky were Warning, and some that looked somewhat safe were also Warning, which made me question whether this is reliable information." |
| | | **Ambiguity / generality of Warning explanations** | Critique that for sites classified as Warning, explanations were more ambiguous ("appears to ~") or offered only generic advice ("be careful since it's suspicious") compared to Safe/Danger, making them less helpful for decision-making. | 6 | "For some Warning tags, no sufficient reason was explained — just 'it's suspicious anyway, so be careful on your own.'" <br> "Since Warning was given to sites the app itself couldn't trust, there were too many ambiguous expressions." |
| | | **Concern over Warning overuse** | Opinion that Warning was classified too often, and that the system should classify more sites clearly as Safe or Danger and reduce Warning where possible. Includes critique that responsibility is excessively shifted to the user. | 4 | "If 'Warning' was attached to it, it felt like Warning could be attached to almost any common site… It seemed to shift too much responsibility onto the consumer." <br> "It felt like Warning was being fired off indiscriminately." |
| **3. Concerns about AI Reliability** | User skepticism about inherent limitations of LLM-based systems. Concerns about hallucination, the impossibility of 100% trust, and the possibility of missing certain threats were the main reasons for lowered trust scores. | **Hallucination concerns** | Responses explicitly mentioning the possibility that AI could fabricate information or make incorrect judgments. Concerns that this could be critical in the security domain. | 7 | "I gave -1 because AI can hallucinate." <br> "Since AI is running underneath, the inevitable hallucination problem keeps coming to mind." |
| | | **Lowered trust due to lack of source / evidence** | Responses that since the system does not specify where it draws information from to make judgments, full trust is hard to grant. Suggestion to present sources together, like Perplexity. | 7 | "I couldn't tell where this answer came from, so I judged the reliability to be insufficient. It would be good to have sources of information." <br> "I didn't know how it works or where it pulls information sources from, so it was hard to fully trust." |
| | | **No 100% trust (inherent limit of security)** | Recognition that no security system can perfectly block all threats. Frequently appears not as a direct critique of the system, but as a general reason for not giving a perfect score. | 6 | "There's no such thing as 100. In security, you can't completely block and detect everything." <br> "I think no app can be 100% trusted. That's why I gave it 90%." |
| | | **Skepticism about hidden-threat detection** | Doubts about whether SafeLink can actually detect deliberately hidden malicious code, newly emerged sites, or sophisticatedly disguised threats. | 3 | "Can it really detect everything perfectly? I had that thought. Can it really find viruses or malware that are deliberately hidden?" <br> "Skepticism about whether this app can really filter out all threats." |
| | | **Self-awareness of automation acceptance** | Self-awareness that as system use prolongs, users tend to follow SafeLink's judgment without carefully reading explanations. Concerns about automation bias. | 2 | "Toward the latter half, I started just following its word, thinking 'I guess this is right.'" <br> "Toward the end, instead of reading explanations, I'd just think 'I guess that's right' and move on. I checked a bit during Warning cases, but later I just blocked them with 'I guess so.'" |
| **4. Autonomy and User Control** | Evaluation of the structure that leaves final decisions to users. Elements such as the separate access button, non-coercive wording, and the existence of the Warning tier were evaluated as reinforcing the feeling that the user retains decision authority. | **User retains final decision authority** | Positive evaluation of the structure where SafeLink only provides information and leaves access decisions entirely to the user. Non-coercive wording and a separate access button preserve autonomy. | 5 | "SafeLink only provided information about the link — whether to access it was left entirely to the user. Full marks." <br> "There was a separate access button. And the wording didn't coercively demand whether to access or not." |
| | | **Distinguishing increased confidence vs. reduced anxiety** | Responses that SafeLink's effect should be seen not as increasing decision confidence per se, but as reducing anxiety or burden during decisions. A subtle distinction between self-efficacy and reassurance. | 4 | "Rather than gaining confidence, it felt more like the criteria for judgment became clearer. Less 'confidence,' more 'helpful.' My anxiety decreased." <br> "It's not SafeLink's problem — it's that I lack confidence in my own decisions. My confidence didn't increase. If you said 'reduced anxiety,' I'd give it 7." |
| | | **Evidence-based confidence increase** | Responses that decision confidence increased when SafeLink's analysis aligned with the user's own judgment, or when information volume grew compared to having to judge without information. | 4 | "Previously I had no information about the link, so I had no confidence in my decision. After checking the feedback, I felt more confident with the rationale attached." <br> "With more information, compared to looking only at the URL, I felt relatively more confident." |
| | | **Visual labels biasing decisions** | Responses that since users see Safe/Warning/Danger labels before reading explanations, the labels effectively influence decision-making, making it hard to view as fully autonomous. | 3 | "It clearly assigned tiers, and even Warning ultimately looked negative, so autonomy felt like 50/50 — a bit ambiguous." <br> "Since I first see Safe/Warning/Danger on screen and then decide whether to access, some preconception forms." |
| **5. Suggestions for Improvement** | System improvements explicitly suggested by users. Includes suggestions across various areas such as UI design, information presentation, and additional features. | **Shift to confident tone** | Suggestions to use definitive expressions instead of ambiguous, speculative ones. Particularly emphasized in sensitive domains such as finance. | 4 | "'You shouldn't do this' — that kind of tone is trustworthy. 'It appears to be ~' doesn't give certainty." <br> "If it said 'this site has been reported as a phishing site,' it would feel like 'oh, that's why' — that would be good." |
| | | **UI / visual emphasis improvement** | Suggestions to improve visual design — colors, icons, keyword emphasis — so that Safe/Warning/Danger labels are visible at a glance. | 4 | "Safe/Warning/Danger don't catch the eye well. Emphasizing them would be good. If icons changed color, it might stand out more." <br> "Things like color in warning indicators should also appear." |
| | | **Request for full URL display** | Request to display the full URL — including the original of shortened URLs — rather than only the domain. Intended to give users material for additional verification. | 3 | "URLs should be shown in full." <br> "A feature that shows the full URL is absolutely necessary." |
| | | **Source / evidence display** | Suggestions to display the information sources used as judgment basis. Suggestion to add citations in a Perplexity-like manner. | 3 | "Just disclosing the source of information would raise trust." <br> "It would have been better to show what materials were referenced as judgment basis." |
| | | **Supplementary information / educational content** | Suggestions to provide auxiliary information beyond the simple judgment result, such as phishing-site identification tips, webpage content summaries, and additional explanations. | 3 | "It would be nice to add tips for distinguishing phishing sites in the app." <br> "Additional supplementary explanations like webpage content summaries would also be good." |
| **6. Adoption Intention and Conditions** | Intention to use SafeLink in real life and the conditions for it. Factors such as price (subscription model), friction from added procedures, QR-code usage frequency, and app-installation preference were presented as influences on adoption decisions. | **Price sensitivity / acceptable subscription range** | Explicit opinions on monthly subscription fees. Responses indicated willingness to use within the range of KRW 1,100–3,000, but unwillingness above KRW 4,000. Comparisons to ad-removal fees also appeared. | 5 | "If it's under KRW 2,900 per month, I'd use it. Above KRW 4,000, I definitely wouldn't." <br> "I'd use it for KRW 1,100/month, treating it as an ad-removal cost. KRW 3,000/month would also be fine." |
| | | **Active adoption intention** | Indication of active willingness to use SafeLink, regardless of price or conditions, or based on recognizing its value. | 4 | "I'd absolutely use it." <br> "I'd say 'why hasn't there been something like this before?' — it's an app we really need." |
| | | **Friction from added procedures** | Concern that SafeLink may add a step to fast-paced usage flows, causing frustration or inconvenience. The issue is the addition of a procedure rather than speed itself. | 2 | "Since one process is added in the end, if users feel inconvenience or frustration, they might just delete the app. The issue isn't speed — it's that one process is inserted in the middle." <br> "People might just go in even on Warning cases out of laziness." |
| | | **App-installation preference / QR usage frequency** | Responses with low active adoption intention because of reluctance to install separate apps or because they rarely use QR codes in daily life. | 2 | "I don't really like installing apps on my phone, hehe — and I don't encounter QR codes often in daily life." <br> "I generally don't install those kinds of apps, hehe." |
| | | **Positive view of automatic execution / accessibility** | Evaluation that SafeLink running automatically upon QR-code recognition without separate app launch is a major accessibility advantage. | 2 | "When the QR code is recognized, the app runs automatically without needing to launch a separate app — accessibility was really good." <br> "When trying to access a link, if the app pops up automatically, you can verify it without entering the app — convenient." |
| **7. Usability and Reduced Verification Burden** | Evaluation that SafeLink reduced cognitive and operational burden by performing link-verification processes (URL analysis, official-site verification, external scanning sites, etc.) that users previously performed manually. Includes automatic execution, intuitive classification, and accessibility for less-engaged users. | | | 13 | "Normally I'd have to look up suspicious links, scan them somewhere else, and then access — but the app removed that tedious process, which was great." <br> "Originally I had to judge based only on the link address, but now the app analyzes it and provides a primary judgment as Safe/Warning/Danger, so the stress of judging on my own decreased." |
| **8. Value for Vulnerable Populations** | Responses that SafeLink would be particularly useful for digitally vulnerable populations — older adults, children, general non-experts, and acquaintances lacking security knowledge. Many responses expressed willingness to recommend it to parents or others rather than themselves. | | | 8 | "It would be more useful for older or elderly adults than for tech-savvy people in their 20s and 30s." <br> "It would especially help people who enter sites without knowing which are security-vulnerable, or who can't tell suspicious sites just by looking." |
| **9. Behavioral Response to the Warning Tier** | Responses about how users encountering the 'Warning' label interpreted it and what behaviors (access, avoidance, additional verification, etc.) followed. Captures how the system's intended delegation of gray-zone judgment was actually received and translated into usage patterns. | **Interpreting Warning as user-responsibility zone** | Responses that take 'Warning' as a gray zone where the system has withheld judgment, and accept it as a domain in which users must judge themselves by mobilizing additional information and context. Closest to the usage pattern SafeLink intended. | 5 | "I felt autonomy because it asked the user to decide whether to access on their own." <br> "I figured Warning-level wouldn't be dangerous even if I clicked." |
| | | **Interpreting Warning as closer to Danger** | Tendency to take the 'Warning' label as effectively a danger signal and behave conservatively (not access). The label functions as a conservative signal, but users do not distinguish its definitional difference from Danger. | 4 | "If it says it's even a little risky, isn't it better not to enter?" <br> "Because of Warning, I couldn't help but hesitate." |
| | | **Need for additional verification on Warning cases** | Responses that for 'Warning' classifications, SafeLink alone was insufficient and additional searches or checks were needed separately. Partial trust in the system. | 2 | "For 'Warning' cases, I felt I needed to look into things a bit more." <br> "It was good because it told me which direction I should search." |
| | | **Need for finer-grained tiers** | Suggestion that the current single 'Warning' tier needs further subdivision. Derived from the evaluation that Warning closer to Danger and Warning closer to Safe are bundled together, causing confusion. | 1 | "It would have been good to have one more tier." |
